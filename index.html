Why the days are shaped this way

The light does the planning

In August the Icelandic sun tracks low and shallow rather than dropping vertically. Golden hour lasts ninety minutes or more instead of sixty, and there are two of them a day, separated by a long, flat, crowded middle. So we shoot at the edges and drive through the middle.

Raufarhólshellir, the lava tunnel1 hour, book the 14:00 slot

Fifty minutes from the airport on Route 39, which makes it the ideal first stop: an hour underground, no hiking, and it breaks the drive east while everyone is still stiff from the plane. A 1,360-metre lava tube formed by the Leitahraun eruption 5,200 years ago — up to thirty metres wide and ten high, with a lit footbridge running through the main chamber and walls striped red, orange and blue by mineral deposits. It holds 2–4°C year-round, so keep a fleece accessible in the boot rather than buried. Tours run hourly; book ahead in August.

Kerið crater30 minutes

A fifty-five metre volcanic caldera with near-vertical red scoria walls and an opaque aquamarine lake at the bottom. Walk the rim, then take the steps down to the water. Overcast actually helps — the red rock saturates.

Geysir45 minutes

Geysir itself is dormant, but Strokkur beside it erupts every six to ten minutes to fifteen or twenty metres. Stand with the sun behind you and wait out two or three cycles. The water domes into a blue bubble about a second before it goes, and that is the photograph.

Gullfoss1 hour

A two-tier fall dropping thirty-two metres into a canyon. Lower platform first, then the upper path, where evening sun puts a rainbow in the spray. Bring a cloth for the lens.

Eating

Grab something at Keflavík before setting off — there is little between the airport and the tunnel. Dinner in Torfhús's own dining room, set Nordic menu, reserved with the room.

Þingvellir is not on today. It has moved to tomorrow morning, where it belongs alongside Silfra — the fissure sits inside the national park, so you get the rift from above and below in the same few hours instead of walking the gorge jet-lagged.

Night 1
Torfhús Retreat

Twenty-five turf-roofed suites built on Viking principles, each with its own basalt geothermal tub looking across the volcanic plain to Langjökull. The most distinctive place to stay in South Iceland and the right way to begin.

Silfra3 hours, 09:00 slot

The reason today is shaped the way it is. Silfra is the flooded fissure where the North American and Eurasian plates pull apart, filled with glacial meltwater that has spent decades filtering through porous lava before it arrives — visibility routinely over a hundred metres, and drinkable mid-swim. You float through in a drysuit over your own thermal layers; roughly thirty to forty minutes in the water, which sits at 2°C, and the colour and light are concentrated in the top few metres, so snorkellers see more than divers do.

What to check before booking

Minimum age 12, minimum height 150 cm, minimum weight 45 kg, and everyone signs a medical statement. All three clear the age comfortably; the 13-year-old should be measured against the height and weight before you pay for five places, because the drysuit sizing is the real gate. Some operators set 14 rather than 12 — book one that runs from 12.

Þingvellir1.5 hours, straight afterwards

You are already inside the park, so walk the Almannagjá gorge down from the upper car park to Öxarárfoss with the fissure fresh in mind. The wall on your left is the literal edge of a continent, and the Alþingi met on this ground from 930 AD. Doing it after Silfra rather than before is the right order — the geology means considerably more once you have been inside it.

Seljalandsfoss and Gljúfrabúi1.5 hours, arrive 19:30

A sixty-metre fall you can walk behind. It faces west, so in the evening the sun sits inside the water curtain and the whole thing lights up from within. This is why we drive south in the afternoon rather than sightseeing — we want to be standing behind it at eight, not at noon with two hundred other people. Everyone gets soaked. Then five minutes north to Gljúfrabúi, hidden inside a slot canyon, where you wade a shallow stream in and shoot up at the shaft of light.

Eating

Friðheimar for lunch on the way south — inside a geothermally heated tomato greenhouse near Reykholt. Tomato soup with endless sourdough, tomato beer, tomato schnapps. Books out weeks ahead; reserve the day we fix dates. Dinner at Restaurant 4 — Icelandic lamb, langoustine, reindeer, deep wine list. Eat at 21:30 after Seljalandsfoss rather than rushing it.

The Secret Lagoon comes out to make room. No great loss — Vök, Mývatn and Hofsós are all still on the route, and Sky Lagoon is there on the last day if you want a fourth.

Night 2
Hotel Rangá

The best countryside hotel in South Iceland. Continent-themed suites, an on-site observatory, and Restaurant 4 — the finest food we will eat outside Reykjavík.

Skógafoss1 hour, arrive by 09:30

Sixty metres wide with twenty-five metres of drop, and it faces south — so the sun is behind you through the late morning and a full rainbow sits in the spray. Shoot from the base for scale, then climb the 527 steps for the view from the lip.

Sólheimajökull glacier walk3 hours with a guide

An outlet tongue of Mýrdalsjökull, crevassed and streaked with volcanic ash. This is the midday activity — harsh light doesn't matter on blue ice, and nobody walks on a glacier unguided, ever. All three are past the usual minimum age of eight to ten, so the whole group goes.

Dyrhólaey1 hour, 17:00

A promontory with a sea arch big enough to sail a boat through and a lighthouse on top. Puffins nest here through mid-August and usually leave in the second half of the month. The upper viewpoint looks east down the whole black coastline.

Reynisfjara1 hour, 19:00

The hexagonal basalt columns and the Reynisdrangar sea stacks, warm in evening light with the crowds thinning.

Eating

Smiðjan Brugghús in Vík — a brewpub doing the best burgers on the south coast, no reservations, go early. Suður-Vík for a proper sit-down dinner. The Soup Company for a fast, warm lunch.

The one genuinely dangerous stop on the route. Sneaker waves at Reynisfjara have killed visitors, including in recent years — they arrive without warning and drag people off their feet. Nobody turns their back on the water, nobody stands on wet sand, and we read the danger board before walking down. This applies to everyone regardless of age, and the confident swimmers among us least of all exempt.

Night 3
Hótel Kría, Vík

Modern, built in 2018, walkable to the black beach and the best-placed property in the village. Vík has no true luxury option — this is the top of the market there.

Fjaðrárgljúfur canyon1.5 hours

A hundred-metre-deep, two-kilometre serpentine gorge carved by a glacial river, with a rim path and platforms. Deep canyons need high sun to reach the floor, so this is a genuine midday site.

Eldhraun lava field30 minutes, roadside

The moss-covered aftermath of the Laki eruption of 1783–84, which killed a quarter of Iceland's population and cooled the entire Northern Hemisphere. The moss takes decades to recover from a single footprint — photograph from the pull-offs and stay off it.

Skaftafell and Svartifoss2.5 hours

A 1.5 km walk uphill to a waterfall framed by black hexagonal basalt columns — the same shapes that inspired the ceiling of Hallgrímskirkja. With this group the longer loop is realistic: continue past the falls to Sjónarnípa for the view straight down onto Skaftafellsjökull, which adds about ninety minutes and is far better than the waterfall alone.

Eating

Systrakaffi at Kirkjubæjarklaustur for lunch. Jón Ríki, a farmhouse restaurant with its own brewery east of Hof. Brunnhóll for farm-made ice cream. Book the Fosshotel dinner table when we book the room — it fills.

Night 4
Fosshotel Glacier Lagoon

The best hotel in the glacier zone by a distance — wood-clad, floor-to-ceiling glass, sauna and hot tubs, positioned exactly between Skaftafell and Jökulsárlón. The hardest booking on the whole route.

Alarm at 04:45

This is the early morning that justifies itself. Expect resistance; it will not survive first sight of the lagoon.

Jökulsárlón glacier lagoon2 hours, 05:30–07:30

Icebergs calved from Breiðamerkurjökull drifting through a tidal lagoon toward the sea. At sunrise the sun comes up over the ocean to the east and lights the ice from behind — the bergs glow blue and there is nobody there. By ten there are four coach parks full.

Diamond Beach1 hour

Directly across the road. Bergs wash out through the channel and strand on black sand, backlit by the low sun. Shoot low and fast as the waves recede; a falling tide leaves the most ice.

Fjallsárlón1 hour

The smaller, quieter lagoon ten kilometres west, with the glacier wall much closer to you. Fewer people and arguably the better composition.

Zodiac among the icebergs1 hour, midday

The RIB takes you right in among the bergs and out to the glacier face — better than the amphibious boat, and the single best hour of the trip for most people who do it.

Vestrahorn at Stokksnes2 hours, from 19:30

A 454-metre serrated massif rising straight out of a black sand spit, with tidal flats that mirror it when the water is still. The finest single photographic location in Iceland, at its best in evening light — and, tellingly, absent from every packaged Ring Road tour. There is a mock Viking film-set village on site. The wind can be severe: weight the tripod.

Eating

Pakkhús in Höfn — a restored 1932 warehouse on the harbour, and the best table in the langoustine capital. Otto Matur & Drykkur is the more elegant alternative. Íshúsið Pizzeria for something fast after Stokksnes.

Night 5
Hótel Höfn

The best in town, comfortable and well run rather than luxurious, and fifteen minutes from Vestrahorn. Honest note: the Höfn market tops out here.

The day people skip

And shouldn't. The road threads fjord after fjord with almost no traffic, sheer walls on one side and water on the other, and reindeer on the slopes — East Iceland is the only place in the country they live wild.

Petra's Stone Collection, Stöðvarfjörður45 minutes

One woman's lifetime accumulation of Icelandic minerals, filling a house and its garden. Odd, personal, and unexpectedly moving — the sort of place nobody expects to like and everybody does.

Seyðisfjörður2 hours, over the Fjarðarheiði pass

The detour of the trip. A blue-and-white wooden church at the end of a rainbow-painted street, sheer mountains, waterfalls down every wall. The pass climbs hard and the view descending into the fjord is the postcard.

Vök Baths1.5 hours, evening

Floating geothermal pools set into Lake Urriðavatn — the only pools in Iceland fed by certified drinkable hot spring water. There is a cold plunge straight into the lake, which is the part that gets photographed. Go at sunset.

Eating

Norð Austur Sushi & Bar in Seyðisfjörður — summer only, fish landed hours earlier, one of the best meals on the island. Skaftfell Bistro for something casual. In Egilsstaðir, Nielsen Restaurant is the fine-dining anchor, though it was closed and due to reopen in summer 2026 — confirm before counting on it. Glóð and Salt Café are the reliable alternatives.

Night 6
Lake Hótel Egilsstaðir

A converted farmhouse on Lagarfljót with a genuinely excellent restaurant that grows its own wasabi. Hótel Hallormsstaður, in Iceland's largest forest, is the more scenic alternative.

Hengifoss1.5 hours, early start

Iceland's third-highest waterfall, twenty minutes from the hotel on the west shore of Lagarfljót. A 2.5 km uphill walk, and the point of it is the cliff behind: alternating basalt layers striped with bands of red clay, each band a separate ancient eruption. Litlanesfoss on the way up is ringed by basalt columns.

Stuðlagil canyon2–4 hours depending on which side

A gorge in Jökuldalur where the river runs an improbable glacial teal between walls of vertical basalt columns — the densest concentration of columnar basalt in Iceland, and only revealed around 2016 when a hydroelectric scheme upstream lowered the water. The west side gives you a viewing platform ten minutes from the car. The east side, from Grund, is 5 km each way along the canyon rim and puts you down at the water among the columns — genuinely the better view, and with this group it is a realistic choice rather than a theoretical one. Decide on the morning based on weather and how day 6 went.

Möðrudalur30 minutes

The highest farm in Iceland, on the Möðrudalsöræfi plateau — a high volcanic desert that looks like Mars. Stop for coffee and kleinur.

Dettifoss1.5 hours

Europe's most powerful waterfall by volume — 193 cubic metres a second falling forty-four metres into the Jökulsárgljúfur canyon. Take Road 862, the paved west bank; the east bank is rough gravel. Selfoss is a fifteen-minute walk upstream and is the more beautiful of the two.

Námafjall Hverir1 hour

A sulphurous field of boiling grey mud pots and roaring steam vents in ochre and rust-coloured earth, with no vegetation at all. Stay on the marked paths — the crust is thin and the mud is above eighty degrees.

Mývatn Nature Baths2 hours, from 19:30

Milky blue, far less crowded than the Blue Lagoon, looking out over the lake. Sunset here is the right way to end the day.

Eating

Vogafjós Farm Resort is the destination meal in the north — farm lamb and trout, their own dairy, and hverabrauð: dense rye bread slow-baked for twenty-four hours in the geothermal ground, served with smoked trout and butter. Daddi's Pizza in Reykjahlíð for the casual option.

The longest day on paper — but it ends with two nights in one bed. If it runs long, drop Hengifoss rather than Stuðlagil, and take the west side of the canyon rather than the east. Ásbyrgi and Dimmuborgir have moved to tomorrow, which is what the extra night buys.

Night 7
Fosshótel Mývatn

Modern, floor-to-ceiling lake views, the best-appointed hotel in the region — and we have it for two nights, so nobody packs a bag tomorrow. Vogafjós Farm Resort is the characterful alternative, with the area's best restaurant attached.

Húsavík — whales and puffins together3.5 hours, morning sailing

Book the combined whale-and-puffin sailing rather than whales alone. Both operators run it, it costs marginally more, and it takes in the island colony as well as the bay — which is how puffins get into this trip without costing a day. Skjálfandi is the best whale watching in Europe: humpbacks reliably, minke, white-beaked dolphins, blue whales occasionally. Take the traditional oak schooner over the RIB — steadier platform for long lenses. Morning sailings have the calmest water, and dress far warmer than the air temperature suggests.

A note on timing

Puffins leave the colonies from mid-August. Travel in the first fortnight and this sailing delivers them; travel after the 20th and no rerouting anywhere in Iceland will help. Dyrhólaey on day 3 and Lóndrangar on day 11 are the backup chances.

Ásbyrgi canyon1.5 hours

A horseshoe canyon three and a half kilometres long with hundred-metre walls, forested at the closed end, formed by catastrophic glacial flooding. Norse tradition has it as a hoofprint left by Odin's horse. Drive to the inner car park and take the path to the pond; the rim trail is the longer option if the day is going well.

Dimmuborgir1.5 hours

A labyrinth of lava pillars, arches and hollow chambers east of the lake. A lava lake ran over marshy ground, the trapped steam punched hollow pillars up through it, and when the crust collapsed the pillars stayed standing. Take the Kirkjan loop.

Skútustaðagígar pseudo-craters1 hour

Grass-covered crater cones on the south shore that were never volcanoes at all — they formed where lava ran over wet ground and the trapped steam blew through. A flat, easy loop full of birdlife, and the gentlest hour of the whole trip.

Grjótagjá30 minutes, optional

A small lava cave with a startlingly blue hot spring inside. Looking only — too hot to bathe, and on private land.

Eating

Lunch in Húsavík: Heimabakarí for baked goods, or fish and chips by the harbour. Back at Mývatn, whichever of Vogafjós or Daddi's Pizza you did not do last night.

Night 8
Fosshótel Mývatn — second night

The one day nobody moves rooms. After seven consecutive hotels that matters more than it sounds, and it is the single best thing the packaged tours do that our earlier plan did not.

Goðafoss1 hour

The waterfall of the gods — a twelve-metre horseshoe where the lawspeaker Þorgeir threw his pagan idols after Iceland adopted Christianity in the year 1000. The west bank gives the classic wide view, the east the intimate one.

Zipline Akureyri2 hours, 12:00 slot

Five ziplines strung across Glerárgil, the wooded river canyon that cuts straight through the middle of Akureyri, connected by short forest paths. The base is on Þingvallastræti, in town — no detour at all. The last line is the longest in northern Iceland at around 260 metres, and the guides teach tricks on the way down if anyone wants them. Minimum age 8 and 30–120 kg, so the whole group rides. This replaces the town wander rather than adding to the day.

Akureyri1 hour over lunch

Nineteen thousand people at the head of a long fjord. Lunch in the centre, and Brynja for ice cream — same recipe since 1939, and worth the queue.

Tröllaskagi, the Peninsula of the Trollsthe afternoon

The coast road north through Dalvík and Ólafsfjörður, tunnelling between fjords under mountains that fall straight into the sea. This is the stretch the packaged tours include and our earlier plan drove straight past.

Siglufjörður and the Herring Era Museum1.5–2 hours

Iceland's northernmost town, once the herring capital of the Atlantic and briefly one of the busiest ports in Europe. The museum spreads across three restored buildings and a boat: the salting stations, the machinery, and the quarters of the 'herring girls' who came north for the season. Far better than it sounds.

Hofsós swimming pool1 hour

A geothermal pool cut into the cliff edge above Skagafjörður, built so the water appears to run straight off into the fjord. An infinity pool with a view of the open sea, for the price of a municipal swim.

Eating

Rub23 in Akureyri for sushi and rubbed fish, or Strikið for the fifth-floor fjord view. Hannes Boy on the Siglufjörður harbour. Dinner at Tindastóll's own restaurant in Sauðárkrókur.

If the day is running late, Hofsós is the cut. The zipline is booked and the museum closes at five; the pool is open until eight and will still be there another time.

Night 9
Hótel Tindastóll, Sauðárkrókur

Built in 1884 and claimed as the oldest hotel in Iceland — timber, small, with a sauna and hot tub. The splurge alternative is genuinely on the route rather than a detour: Deplar Farm sits in the Fljót valley between Siglufjörður and Skagafjörður.

Glaumbær turf farm1 hour

An eighteenth-century turf-and-timber farmhouse complex, gable ends facing the field, interiors intact. The best-preserved example in Iceland and the clearest picture anyone will get of how Icelanders actually lived. The café serves cream cakes on period china.

Kolugljúfur canyon1 hour

A gorge in Víðidalur where the river drops through a series of falls, named for the giantess Kola who was said to live in it. Seven kilometres of gravel off the Ring Road, free parking, and almost nobody there.

Hvítserkur1 hour

A fifteen-metre basalt sea stack shaped like a drinking dragon, standing offshore on the Vatnsnes peninsula. Best at low tide with wet sand reflecting it — check the tide table before committing. Seals haul out nearby.

Eiríksstaðir1 hour

A reconstructed Viking longhouse in Haukadalur, on the site where Erik the Red lived and where Leif Eriksson is believed to have been born — the family that reached Greenland and then North America five centuries before Columbus. A costumed guide tells the saga round the fire pit. Visitors rate it one of the best cultural stops in the country, and it sits on the road to Snæfellsnes rather than off it.

Stykkishólmurevening

A harbour town of brightly painted timber houses on a basalt bluff, looking out over Breiðafjörður and its two thousand islands. Climb Súgandisey, the little island reached by a causeway, for the view back over the roofs at sunset.

Eating

The Glaumbær café for cream cakes at eleven. Sjávarpakkhúsið on the Stykkishólmur harbour for dinner — mussels pulled from Breiðafjörður that morning, served in the pot. Narfeyrarstofa in a restored timber house is the more formal alternative.

The longest driving day, and the one to trim if the weather turns. Kolugljúfur and Eiríksstaðir are the optional pair — dropping both brings the day to about 300 km and 3h30.

Night 10
Fosshotel Stykkishólmur

On the hill above the harbour with views over Breiðafjörður, and the practical choice for five — it has proper family rooms. Hótel Egilsen, a restored 1867 timber house on the waterfront, is the boutique alternative.

The easiest day of the trip

Ninety kilometres of peninsula holding a glacier, lava fields, black beaches, basalt cliffs and fishing villages. Short drives, long stops — and ten days into the road, that is the point.

Kirkjufell and Kirkjufellsfoss1.5 hours

A 463-metre nunatak — a peak that stood above the ice sheet while the glaciers ground away the land around it — and the most photographed mountain in Iceland. The classic composition puts the small waterfall in the foreground, two minutes from the paid car park. Morning light works from the waterfall side. Climbing it is no longer permitted.

Saxhóll crater45 minutes

A scoria cone with a metal staircase to the rim. Five minutes up, and the view over Snæfellsjökull and the coast is out of all proportion to the effort.

Djúpalónssandur and Dritvík1.5 hours

A black pebble beach reached through a lava field, with the rusted ribs of the English trawler Epine still scattered across the sand where it wrecked in 1948. Fourteen crew died and the wreckage is left as a memorial, so it stays where it lies. Four lifting stones sit above the beach — 23, 54, 100 and 154 kg — which fishermen once used to test whether a man was strong enough for a boat crew. The 54 kg 'weakling' was the threshold for getting hired. Expect competition.

Lóndrangar1 hour

Two basalt plugs standing straight out of the sea, the remains of a crater the ocean has eaten away, with fulmars, kittiwakes and guillemots on the ledges — and a late chance at puffins. Viewing platforms five minutes from the road.

Arnarstapi and Hellnar1.5 hours

Basalt arches and stacks full of nesting birds, and a 2.5 km coastal path between the two villages — worth walking one way and being collected at the other end. Above it sits Snæfellsjökull, the glacier-capped volcano Jules Verne used as the entrance to the centre of the earth.

Búðakirkjaevening

A small black timber church standing alone on a lava field by the sea, with a graveyard dating to 1705. It is a two-minute walk from tonight's hotel, which means we can wait for the light rather than photograph it at noon with a coach party.

Eating

Bjargarsteinn in Grundarfjörður for lunch, looking straight across at Kirkjufell. Dinner at Hótel Búðir, which is half the reason to stay there — book the table with the room.

Night 11
Hótel Búðir

Alone on the lava field at the peninsula's southern edge, beside the black church, looking across the bay to the glacier. One of the most romantic hotels in Iceland, with a restaurant worth planning the evening around.

Deildartunguhver30 minutes

Europe's highest-flow hot spring, discharging 180 litres a second at ninety-seven degrees — it heats the towns for sixty kilometres around. Steam everywhere, and a farm shop selling tomatoes grown on it.

Hraunfossar and Barnafoss1.5 hours

Meltwater seeps out of a lava field in a kilometre-wide curtain of small falls into a turquoise river — not one waterfall but a thousand, emerging from under the rock along the whole bank. Barnafoss is a short walk upstream, narrower and violent. Late-afternoon side-light here is one of the most underrated shots in the country.

An infinity-edge geothermal lagoon on the ocean edge with a seven-step ritual — better designed than the Blue Lagoon and ten minutes from the city. The fourth bath of the trip if you want it.

Reykjavíkevening

Hallgrímskirkja at the top of the Rainbow Street, the Sun Voyager on the shore, Harpa on the harbour. Whatever is left of the trip, spend it walking.

Eating

Dill — Iceland's Michelin-starred restaurant, New Nordic tasting menu, booked a month ahead. Sæta Svínið is the easier option. Bæjarins Beztu for the hot dog, a genuine national institution at about four euros.

The car

Five people with luggage means a seven-seater or a large SUV, automatic, booked well ahead — the big vehicles go first in August. A 2WD is technically enough for this route since we use no F-roads.

Take gravel protection and sand-and-ash protection. Neither is included as standard. The second covers a real risk on the south coast, where ash storms can strip the paint off a car in minutes and the bill runs into thousands. And wind is the real hazard in Iceland, not ice — never open a car door into a gust. It will take the door off, and no policy covers it.

Two adults, three teenagers, twelve nights

What it costs

One structural point first. Almost no Icelandic hotel has a room that sleeps five. The standard is a double or a twin, occasionally a family room for four. For five of us that means two rooms a night at nearly every stop — and that single fact is the largest line in the budget. It roughly doubles the accommodation cost against the same trip for a couple.

Two ways to soften it: ask each property directly for a family room, suite or connecting rooms when booking, or substitute apartments and cottages at five of the stops. Both versions are below, and nothing about the route, the sites or the timing changes between them.

Where the money actually goes

  • Accommodation is roughly half the total and the only line with real leverage. Everything else is close to fixed.
  • Booking early beats every other saving. August rooms priced in February versus June differ by thirty to forty per cent, and the good ones are gone by late spring.
  • Children's pricing is inconsistent. Whale watching and the lagoons discount children heavily; glacier walks and zodiac tours often charge full price and impose minimum ages. Check ages when booking each one.
  • Alcohol is punitive — €60–90 for a bottle of wine with dinner. Buy at the duty-free shop in the arrivals hall at Keflavík before leaving the airport, as everyone in Iceland does.
  • The five booked activities come to roughly €2,750–3,700 for the group. Silfra is the largest single line and the one worth paying for; the lava tunnel is the cheapest thing on the list and slots into the drive from the airport.
Before we go

Worth knowing

  • Twelve nights, thirteen calendar days. Day 12 ends in Reykjavík and we fly home on day 13.
  • Book Silfra the moment dates are fixed, and measure before you do. Minimum age 12, minimum 150 cm and 45 kg. The 13-year-old is the one to check — the drysuit sizing is the real gate, not the age. Pick an operator that runs from 12 rather than 14.
  • Travel in the first fortnight of August if puffins matter. They leave the colonies from mid-month. The day 8 sailing out of Húsavík is the best chance, with Dyrhólaey and Lóndrangar as backups.
  • No day exceeds 380 km. Days 7 and 10 are the pinch points and both carry optional stops that can be dropped on the morning — Hengifoss takes day 7 to 280 km, and cutting Kolugljúfur with Eiríksstaðir takes day 10 to 300.
  • Northern lights become possible from about 20 August, when the nights get properly dark again. A bonus, not a plan — and it pulls against the puffin timing, so pick one.
  • Reykjanes, where we land, is an active volcanic zone. The last eruption ran 16 July to 5 August 2025 and another is considered likely; none have affected flights, and the Blue Lagoon closes and reopens as a precaution. Check safetravel.is the week we travel.
  • Pack for four seasons in one day — waterproof shell and trousers, fleece, hat and gloves, proper boots, swimwear permanently at the top of the bag, and thermal base layers for Silfra and the lava tunnel specifically.
  • Tap water is among the best in the world. Refillable bottles, never buy bottled. The hot water smells faintly of sulphur; harmless.
  • Everything takes cards, including public toilets and remote petrol pumps. No cash needed anywhere on this route.
